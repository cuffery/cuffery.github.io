*Note: I wrote the below note as I was about to leave Meta (nee Facebook), where I grew up professionally and learned what it meant to be a data professional. I was there from early 2018 to mid 2021.*

I do not think my tenure here is long or illustrious enough to warrant a career story - let me just start by getting this out of the way. This post is not one, and I encourage you to read every single piece in that series, as I’ve found tremendous value in reading on and internalizing lessons (some hard, some ugly, but all inspiring) learned by other data scientists in the org. This post is what I thought I learned in my short time here; and in talking to a few newer folks on the team and sharing some of the nuggets, it seems that there might be a broader appeal to what I’m about to say next. This post might be more helpful to folks in early career (maybe right out of college), as that was my background when I came into FB, but could also provide some glimpses to managers or externally hired senior folks as well. I tried to bold the things that were most valuable to me, but for those who are too lazy to go through a 19-minute read, here are a few nuggets:
1. (Learn to) Be a working professional
2. Separate work and life
3. Move for the problem domain, Stay for the manager
4. Yell if you start to drown
5. Keep an open mind
6. Have backbone
7. Own your career

## (Learn to) Be a working professional

The first I had to learn right out of college was how different workplaces really are from school, and the new identity “working professional” I had to assume: 
* At school, your school life includes your social life + academics + sports + everything else, and sometimes the same person can be your friend and TA (happened to me, great experience getting some side help) and there is really no clear distinction between you being “ON” or “OFF” - you are always ON. You (or parents) are paying 100% of the cost and therefore, pretty much have the sole discretion on how you want to spent your time and how you want to show up (if ever), with 100% responsibility of the consequences/
* At work, there is a clearer delineation between the time you are getting paid vs. your own (i.e. who owns the means of production here). You also don’t control 100% of the consequence of your labor, since we are now in a large collaborative, sometimes competitive, environment. 
Therefore, the expectations are different, and here’s what I learned about what being a working professional means: 
1. Always Be Constructive (ABC). It’s that simple. No one wants to work with twats or downers, and you don’t want that either. It means taking feedback gracefully (whether you agree or not - that’s a different matter; but you want people to continue to be willing to share feedback with you, good or bad); it means bringing positive energy to work (don’t get frustrated easily or let that frustration show out too much); it means always looking for angles where we can help solve problems; it also means looking for the productive things you can do despite the situation. 
2. Do Your Job. Belichick’s favorite mantra rings true no matter what you think of the Patriots. What is the job that you are paid to do? Are you effective in your job? As a data scientist, while we do wear many hats, I think our primary job is to make sure decision-making is of the highest quality consistently, wherever we are. This closely aligns with Analytics org’s mission: Drive better outcomes using data as a voice for our communities. Are we building the right products for users? Are we using product team’s time and resources most efficiently? Are we keeping the business healthy? Are we holding the team(s) accountable with rigorous metrics and goals? Are we on the right track to execute on our strategy? Are we having the right strategy? Are we building the org right? Analytics comes in with unbiased opinion and relentless focus on impact, and I’m a firm believer that we elevate the decisions around us. Keep doing that more please. 
3. Really an extension from 2. You are paid to do your job, you are not paid to (try to) like everyone and let everyone like you. It doesn’t mean you can be a jerk - jerks are not welcome anywhere and are not constructive; it means you should not spend too much energy worrying about if your coworkers like you, or if your manager likes you (although this one helps a lot). I’ve seen it in myself and in a lot folks with no work experience - we are eager to please and yearn for acceptance into this new social structure (workplace), but in the end my biggest impact never came because so-and-so really liked me or disliked me. 
4. Related to 2 as well - the onus is on us to keep ourselves motivated. There might be days where we don’t feel like going to school - and sometimes we just might decide not to go (I never made it to my classes before 11AM anyways...). However, at work, you need to show up every day, and usually “ not feeling like it” is not a superb reason to skip work especially when you have project reviews or interviews on the calendar or teammates need to be unblocked (though, do take advantage of sick days and mental health days if you need them!). I also think, even if you are working on your life’s passion, it’s unlikely that everyday you come in just motivated and never have ups and downs - maybe you are, but I’m definitely not that type. So I have a few tricks I’ve learned that are helpful me:
    1. Structure your week with blocks and maybe 1x1s with your favorite co-workers so that you always have something to look forward to, other than the weekends: for instances I have my Fridays usually blocked off, and I have Wednesday as an org-level no meeting day. So I always have Wednesday and Friday to look forward to. I also have come to appreciate my DS mentorship circle a whole lot, and look forward to that bi-weekly get-togethers. 
    2. Know super early on what kind of days work best for you - I know from my freshman year Japanese classes that I’m absolutely not a morning person, and I try my absolute best not to do morning meetings (so bye-bye teams that need cross-atlantic or even cross-continent collaboration). When pandemic started, I also took on more work taking care of our dog since my S/O has an insanely busy schedule of a Facebook PM, so I aggressively block off time when I need to walk and feed the dog. I compensate that by working a bit at night, which I actually don’t mind at all and I realized I focus better anyways without distractions. I’d imagine it will be very different for morning people; point is, maybe don’t fight nature or try too hard if it’s just not working for you (I still had morning strength/conditioning sessions in high school and college for rugby, but I most definitely did not miss those). 

## Separate work and life

Maintaining a clear separation between work and life has been one of key adjustments I had to make out of college, and has been the key to maintain my sanity in this fast-moving and sometimes chaotic organization. I have a few tips and tricks that work well for me; YMMV but the crucial part is really to have some kind of separation. After all, you are not really paid enough by Zuck to do this gig 24/7, and I would even argue no work is really worthy enough to occupy your brain 24/7 unless you are blazing trails for the entire humanity (like spacefaring, like fighting global epidemic - but even astronauts and health care workers take days off). 
1. Either have a personal phone that does not have ANY facebook stuff (and turn off consumer Messenger app notifications if you work at Messenger), or have all notifications off especially for Workplace and Workchat and work email apps outside working hours. Generally going from the “push” mode of getting information to the “pull” mode is going to put a lot of autonomy in your hands and better maintain the separation between work and life when you want it, wherever you want it. 
2. Have a ritual at the beginning and the end of your work day to signify the boundary. I close the lid of my work laptop semi-ceremoniously at the end of my workday to tell myself, that’s it, and only open the lid up again the next morning. 
3. Don’t talk about work when you hang out with friends from work. Sometimes we make friends who are also our co-workers, and I usually try not to talk about work (i.e. specific projects) stuff too much (other than gossips and hot takes, which I LOVE). 

## Move for the problem domain, Stay for the manager

I joined my first team Partnership Signas under Ads Signals and Identity (part of Ads Measurement) right out of bootcamp. The first team was interesting and the whole experience taught me good lessons - I joined as I thought the manager (who joined the company three months before me) was approachable and could be a good career coach, and the team was full of expert ICs with established tenures here. In hindsight, there were a few things I did not know (and would not have picked this team had I known): (1) all the senior ICs were thinking about leaving the team (HUGE red flag); (2) the new manager had some tension with some ICs, and was not effective or proven yet; (3) the space (ads signals) was so mature that people were either optimizing around the tiny edges or spending entire cycles dealing with external forces (IDFA, iOS 15, Chrome cookies, etc.), which means there was not many interesting and innovative projects for a n00b like me and much work felt reactive. I then switched to Messenger RTC; after a year I was there, my current manager joined RTC as well (from ads), and I decided to stay in the Messenger RTC domain but report to her - since we were actually ICs together in our first team and I knew she “gets” me and looks out for me. It turns out to be one of the best decisions I’ve made in FB - she coached me effectively, got me promoted to E5 and set me up for success, and I could reap the dividend of staying in the problem domain for a relatively long period of time. 

So I think when it comes to picking teams, especially for people completely new to the company and the industry, here's a few things to look for (in that order), in this constantly-changing and fast-moving environment:
* An interesting problem domain. People change, teams re-org, only problems stay the same. So make sure you pick an area that you clearly want to spend the next year or two learning and developing in. (e.g. ads, e-commerce, real-time communications, virtual reality - these are all different domains).
* * A somewhat tenured manager that you can get along with EASILY - don't ignore red flags or quirks. Bonus points if the manager “gets” you. This is also a point I learned from Joe's career story (one of my all-time favorites as well as the related AMA, strongly recommended) - “Personally, I think the dumbest moves I've made have been to leave places with good supportive managers where I was doing good work. Don't underestimate the value of being happy in the short term and growing at work. It's often better to hang out for 4 years and be promoted than to keep jumping to new things.” 
* A relatively stable team with domain experts that are clearly passionate about the problem domain. Most likely they won't tell you if they are thinking about leaving, but you should grab private 1x1s with them and ask subtly.
* Good XFNs that you can learn from and partner with.

## Yell if you start to drown

In April 2020, I switched teams and went (back) to Aleksey, and was given one of the biggest projects I’ve done here - Project LightSpeed. I joined as a STO for RTC, and took over the overall experimentation when Andy Kreek went on paternity leave. I struggled massively in that expanded scope trying to debug experiment failings, metric regressions, RTC specific problems, and just plainly interfacing with 100s of XFNs (PMs, ENG, leads). Balls were getting dropped, and I was letting my ego get in the way thinking to myself, “I could grind this out if I just work a little harder”. That’s actually extremely risky and ineffective. Fortunately, my manager(s) saw my struggle bus ride and decided to bring Lisa Philips on the bus too to really save the day for me. Immediately things calmed down and improved for both me and the workstreams - she brought with her structure and composure to the project, and really brought the runaway project to the right track. Thank you Lisa! It was actually a blast and total pleasure working with you side by side on LS. And here’s my lessons learned:
1. It's actually incredibly difficult to see it in real-time that you are failing - it's much easier for others to tell though, and that's why we really need managers and mentors to tell us when it's time to course correct and intervene. It's totally okay to ask for help! 
2. More importantly, I recognized the critical skill here to I need to be able to sense I'm drowning and try not to “grind through it”. It would be a lot easier for ME and the project teams had I learned to ask for help and escalate things quickly. 
3. It's also not automatically true that you can do well with expanded scope despite our promos being lagging, because you are constantly given more stuff to do and the scope change can be a step function. It's dangerous to ever become complacent just because you nailed it last half - every half here is a new challenge. 
As an aside, I also felt that working on an org-wide project (like LightSpeed) is probably one of the fastest ways to expand your network at FB and meet cool people inside a large org, and good to raise your profile and visibility. I got to work with VPs and directors, and I got to work with a bunch of crazy talented data scientists and engineers that normally would be a lot harder to band together and find collaboration opportunities with. For more of my learnings on Project LightSpeed, you can read it here. (I’d also recommend Lisa’s masterpiece too)

## Keep an open mind

During my time on Project Lightspeed, I often received asks from leads and higher-ups for which I thought, “there is no way we are able to do it with our current tech”. More often than not, there is actually a (and sometimes multiple) way to do that, and I had to fall on my face to learn a hard lesson. 

For instance, I was once asked to create a dashboard based on Deltoid results for our mega experiments so that all the XFN partners can focus on one convenient source of truth rather than passing a million Deltoid links around. I thought that was impossible because Deltoid3 UI did not say “export to dashboard” back then, and I resented leads for even asking this “stupid” question and blamed them for “not knowing our tech well, unlike us ICs!”. Oh boy was I wrong: 
1. It turns out we could indeed build dashboards based on deltold results (in many ways) - With help from all around, we used Clustoid and built a few dashboards, and Ben and Tyrone took it further and enabled scheduled results. 
2. Moreover, it’s also possible to do country (or really any metric dimension) slice on these dashboards with the right configuration. So never say never and “Make it Happen”! (it was also the closest I felt connected to our “hack” culture)
3. ICs turn out don’t necessarily know more about our tech than managers, and I learned an even bigger lesson in checking my bias and hubris here which is neither constructive nor humble. 
This is not the only incident I’ve experienced in my short time here where I did not think hard enough but let my multitude of biases set in and color my judgment; had I been able to keep an open mind and been more willing to explore, I could perhaps more quicker progress on many of my projects and maintain a much better relationship with folks important to my work. 

## Have backbone, have an opinion

Amazon is one of the most successful companies in the internet era; whether you like it or not, there’s no denying that Bezos-led team has been incredibly consistent in their innovative approach and culture, as manifested in Bezos’ little red book Amazon Leadership Principles. One of the things that I liked most from there is “Have backbone” (you can then disagree but commit, but first you need to have an opinion, and have backbone to back it up). We are literally paid to have data-driven opinions and to explain these opinions to other folks (and hopefully to convince them, which is called “influence” in PSC parlance); there is little value in being “yes (wo)man” to you, to the org, and to our users. There’s a few scenarios you might see your backbone being tested, and I ask that you stay cautious and make a conscious and conscientious decision on what you are going to do: 
1. When your partners/leads want to file a SEV because their goal metric is trending below target, or worse, because a big experiment is showing metric regressions and they wanted to “prioritize” it: is it the right thing to do? Does “filing SEVs” seem the best use of everyone’s time? How can you unblock the team best and be constructive? (I think it’s a giant red flag if you team constantly files SEVs, and worse, throw DS’ weary bodies into them)
2. When you hear people (sometimes higher-ups) say “next quarter/half we need to focus on X/build Y”: is there any data supporting the direction and the why behind it? What does our data say about this direction? To be clear, not all decisions can be made perfectly with data and maths; as Bezos was repeatedly quoted in “Invent and Wander” (by the way, excellent book, which I nicknamed Bezos’ little yellow book by Walter Isaacson), some of his bigger decisions had to be made with guts and principles (in his case, customer obsession as he’s inventing on customers’ behalf). But is every decision you see in the org that kind of decision, or can they be made better with data and rigorous interpretation of data?
3. You might also see a bunch of things that seem to go against our culture; and here I think you should be incentivized and have the backbone to speak up because culture is made up of thousands of decisions that people collectively make. For instance, when you see things trending from “open” to “closed”, or when you see titles being made explicit, or when you see good behavior that you want to encourage, or other millions of examples literally happening daily, have an opinion, and have the backbone to speak and back your opinion up. 
4. Inherently, you need to have opinions on things to substantiate your backbone. I’m not saying you are expected to have an opinion on everything; but I’m saying that for things that matter to you, it’s best to have one. It doesn’t mean every opinion you have will be a good one, but they should ideally all have come from a place of data and reason (but also watch out for “the Righteous Mind” and unconscious bias!) It also doesn’t mean you can’t change your opinion; on the contrary, keeping an open mind and mashing opinions of different people together often produce the strongest decisions in my observation. 

## Own your career

The single most important thing I’ve learned in the past five years since college can be boiled down to this: Own Your Career. It is my career, and it is ultimately my book I’m writing - not my manager’s, not my director’s, not Zuck’s or even Facebook’s (although, we all contribute to and influence one another’s decisions and work). Even though I considered myself to be fairly proactive ever since I was a toddler, I was “lost” in college and workplace, and just looked for the usual career paths people get onto (I went into consulting after college literally because I had no idea what I wanted to do). Even joining Facebook was more of that “seeing what smart people in my company were doing - oh they all went to Facebook to be data scientists! It must be the right thing to do and okay Imma try that too”. But now I see how “blur” I was (to use a Singlish slang word), and I wanted to compile a questionnaire for people in my situation with things I wish I had thought about when I started, so that I could start “owning my career” sooner: 
1. What do you want to do in five years? (goal)
    1. I think 5y is a nice time frame, allowing perhaps for a pivot or two for people to reach their goal
2. What skills and knowledge do you need for that goal? (internal)
3. What help and mentorship do you need for that goal? (external - it tells you the kind of environment you want to find for yourself)
4. What should you optimize for in order to achieve that goal? 

I wrote one initially at the end of 2018 for myself and then updated at the beginning of 2019: 
￼
* I knew I wanted to try startup life and build products that I can be proud of and also meaningful to people and society; but now I don’t think I will be moving back or looking for a job in Singapore anytime soon (not with the pandemic still going, at least). Instead, I now think about the skills I needed for that world, and think about what I needed to learn next and where can I learn that best. 
* I did finish my part-time masters at UW PMP (motivation was that I wanted to have a more structured education on what’s the latest in the computer science field and learn more about machine learning); I did get married to the most wonderful person in the world; I did get A LOT better of snowboarding (no parks or backcountry, but I could hang with the big boys and girls)
* I had no idea why I was so fascinated with Google; HRT was because I had a few friends there, and I was told competitive athletes with data science backgrounds might be a good fit for trading. I also did not become an intern manager, though I did become a bootcamp mentor and also mentored a few newer folks on my team. 
* Even subconsciously, I was doing the 50/50 goals all along...

## It’s the People

￼
One of the most frequently asked questions during interviews is “what do you like about Facebook”; and without hesitation I tell them it’s really the people. We have the most consistent pool of talents in the industry for DS in my opinion, and our ENG and XFN teams are world class.
